# 3.10 Scoring to picture

Built at risk while the owner was away (plan item B7), on top of the
video clips (3.9) and timecode and hit points (3.10, A12). Screenshots
from the cloud (a made-up test pattern on Linux: a tiny film whose
colour changes each frame), in `/mnt/project-files/screenshots/`:
`app-video-scoring.png` (the Video pane) and `app-video-export.png`
(Export with the video).

The copy of the video is written by each system's own media framework,
as reading is. **On Windows it isn't offered yet:** the first version,
tried on CI's build machines, wrote copies whose picture was two frames
late and damaged until the next key frame, which would make scoring to
them wrong. The option is greyed out there ("Not on this system yet")
until that is fixed; the code is kept as the starting point. On Linux it
works with only the media framework's base set installed.

## On the PC

- [ ] **3.10 The filmstrip comes back at once.** Drop a film of a few
      minutes, let "Filmstrip n%" reach the end, then close the app and
      open the project again (or drop the same film in another project).
      Pass: the filmstrip is all there straight away, with no
      "Filmstrip n%" counting. Saved copies of the film, and one that
      came in a bundle, find it too, as it is known by the file's
      contents.
- [ ] **3.10 Timecode on the picture.** Open the Video pane (View →
      Video). Screenshot: `app-video-scoring.png`. Pass: the picture
      has the timecode at its foot, large, like a burnt-in timecode, and
      the film's name, frame number and time at its top left. It reads
      the same as the transport bar's timecode. Untick "Timecode" in the
      pane's row (or use "Timecode on the picture" in the command
      search): the large timecode goes, the small line stays.
- [ ] **3.10 Timecode from the film.** With a film in and the timecode
      not yet set up, press "Timecode from the film" in the pane's row
      (also in Edit). Pass: the frame rate becomes the film's (23.976,
      24, 25, 29.97, 30, 50 or 60; at 29.97 drop-frame stays if it was
      chosen) and the start timecode falls on the film's first frame, so
      the timecode on the picture counts up from the start timecode as
      the film plays. If your editor burnt timecode into the film, the
      two should agree to the frame. The button goes once lined up.
- [ ] **3.10 A frame at a time.** Press `.` and `,` (or ◀ ▶ in the
      pane's row, or Transport → On a frame / Back a frame). Pass: the
      picture moves exactly one frame each press, the frame number on
      the picture goes up or down by one, playback stops if it was
      running, and it stops at the film's first and last frames. Away
      from any film, it steps by the timecode's frames.
- [ ] **3.10 Hit points on the picture.** Add a few hit points (H) at
      moments in the film and play through them. Pass: as the playhead
      reaches one, "Hit point: its name" shows on the picture for most
      of a second; top right, "Next: name in 1.25 s" counts down to the
      next one. The strip under the picture shows the film's length with
      a diamond for each hit point; click one to go there, or anywhere
      on the strip to go to that point of the film. `]` and `[` (or the
      pane's ⏮◆ ◆⏭) go to the next and previous hit point.
- [ ] **3.10 Moving the film moves its hit points.** Drag the filmstrip
      along. Pass: the hit points on it move with it, and if the
      timecode was lined up with the film it moves too, so the picture,
      its sound, its hit points and its timecode stay together. Ctrl+Z
      puts them all back.
- [ ] **3.10 Export the video with the mix (Linux only for now).** File → Export the video
      with the mix (or tick "Also a copy of the video … with this mix"
      in the Export window). Screenshot: `app-video-export.png`. Pass:
      the Exports folder gets "<film> with the mix.mp4" within moments
      of the mix rendering; it plays in your usual video player with
      the mix (not the film's own sound) in step with the picture from
      the first frame to the last, and the picture looks exactly as
      sharp as the original (it is copied, not re-encoded). Try a film
      that is not at the start of the song: the mix in the copy starts
      at the film's first frame.
## Decisions to accept

- [ ] **The system writes the copy.** As for reading, each system's own
      media framework puts the mix into the copy: no codec code in the
      app. The picture is copied as it is; only the sound is encoded.
- [ ] **What kind of file the copy is.** On Linux the same kind of
      file as the film: MP4 gets AAC if the
      system has an encoder for it, or else MP3 at 320 kbit/s; MOV and
      AVI get the mix uncompressed, as video editors like; MKV gets
      FLAC; WebM and Ogg get Opus.
      The Windows version, once fixed, would write MP4 with AAC.
- [ ] **The whole film, from its first frame.** The copy is always the
      whole film, as the picture can't be cut without re-encoding it,
      and the mix is rendered for exactly its length, so music past the
      film's end is left out.
- [ ] **The film's own sound is left out of the copy, unless it is in
      the mix.** Its sound is an ordinary track: mute it to leave it out,
      or leave it playing to keep dialogue under the music.
- [ ] **Filmstrips are kept in the app's cache folder,** beside the
      converted audio files, up to 1 GB in all; the ones used least
      recently are let go of first. A long film's strip takes about
      20 to 30 MB.
- [ ] **Keys.** `.` and `,` step a frame, `]` and `[` go to the next and
      previous hit point. All can be changed in Settings → Key commands.

## Every action is a command

On a frame (`frame-forward`), Back a frame (`frame-back`), Next hit point
(`next-hit-point`), Previous hit point (`previous-hit-point`), Timecode
on the picture (`video-timecode`), Timecode from the film
(`timecode-from-film`) and Export the video with the mix
(`export-video`) are all in the command table, so the command search,
keys, and later the agent interface run them the same way.

Known limits: trimming a video clip's ends isn't built yet, so the copy
always covers the whole film. The timeline's snapping doesn't snap to
the film's frames yet. A film at a rate timecode doesn't count in (such
as 12 or 15 fps) keeps the timecode rate already chosen, with its start
lined up.
